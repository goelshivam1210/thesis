\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:pho_motivation}

The results in \cref{ch:flex} show that an object-centric force representation can separate an articulated manipulation skill from the robot that executes it. We now ask how far the same principle can be carried when the object is not constrained by a joint. The setting is rigid-body transport: a robot must push an object along a desired path without knowing its mass or the friction between the object and the supporting surface.

This problem removes a source of structure that \FLEX{} could exploit. A drawer or door moves along a prismatic or revolute joint, so its allowable motion is geometrically constrained. A rigid object on a plane can both translate and rotate, and its response to contact depends on the resistance it encounters. The same command that moves a light box smoothly across one floor may barely move a heavier box on another; an unsuitable force direction can also cause the object to rotate or drift away from the path. Successful transport therefore requires a policy that represents progress relative to the path while adjusting its forces to the current interaction regime.

The framework developed in this chapter retains the object-centric separation between policy and robot control. It learns a policy in simulation that maps a path-relative object state to planar boundary forces. The state is conditioned on a normalized descriptor of contact resistance, and randomization over mass and friction exposes the policy to different resistance regimes during training. At deployment, the robot does not identify mass and friction separately. Instead, a short impedance-based probing routine uses proprioceptive force measurements to estimate the descriptor, and a compliant controller realizes the policy's requested forces. The descriptor provides a coarse indication of the interaction regime rather than a complete physical model.

From the dissertation's perspective, this is a second instance of assimilation through interaction abstraction. Mass and friction are treated as dimensions of variation within a fixed policy representation. The robot estimates where the current interaction lies within that representation and conditions its behavior accordingly; it does not learn a new executor or update the policy parameters at deployment. The experiments examine how well this representation generalizes beyond its training conditions, including changes in mass, friction, object dimensions, and path geometry. The learned policy is also deployed without policy retraining on a Boston Dynamics Spot mobile manipulator under two payload conditions, although robot-specific impedance gains are selected to maintain stable contact.

The claim is necessarily scoped. The method addresses quasi-static planar transport through sustained, single-point boundary contact, and its scalar descriptor cannot express every possible change in contact dynamics. Within that scope, the chapter tests whether an appropriate interaction abstraction can absorb physical variation that would otherwise make a learned manipulation policy brittle.

The remainder of the chapter reviews learning-based and model-based approaches to pushing and sim-to-real transfer. It then presents the object-centric policy, interaction-estimation procedure, and robot execution layer, followed by evaluations of parameter and path generalization, the role of the interaction descriptor, and physical deployment.
