\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:flex_motivation}

The previous chapter studied what an agent can do after novelty invalidates an existing behavior. By using action abstractions to identify the failed behavior, the agent could learn a replacement while preserving the rest of its competence. This chapter asks the complementary question: \emph{can an interaction be represented so that changes in the object or robot do not require the policy to be relearned in the first place?} In the terminology of \cref{ch:novelty_open_world}, the focus shifts from localized accommodation after failure to assimilation through generalization.

Consider a skill for opening a drawer. At the level of appearance and purpose, a drawer differs from a dishwasher rack or a cylindrical rod sliding inside a sleeve. At the level relevant to manipulation, however, all three can share the same structure: motion is constrained to translation along a prismatic axis. If a policy represents this common structure---the joint axis, the current displacement, and the force needed to produce motion along it---then the three objects need not be treated as unrelated manipulation problems. They can be handled as different instances of the same underlying interaction. The same reasoning applies to revolute mechanisms such as doors and hinged lids.

This observation connects task structure directly to assimilation. An abstraction that captures whether an interaction is prismatic or revolute allows variation in an object's appearance, geometry, and semantic category to be interpreted within a structure the policy already knows how to manipulate. The relevant question is then not whether the agent has encountered that particular drawer, rack, rod, or door before, but whether it can identify the articulation and instantiate the existing policy for its parameters. Similarly, the physical requirement does not change merely because a Panda robot is replaced by a UR5. The task remains to produce an appropriate force at the object, although the robot-specific commands required to realize that force will differ.

A policy expressed directly in robot joint angles, end-effector motions, or motor torques can obscure both sources of commonality. It may entangle the task's articulation structure with the geometry of a particular object and with the kinematics, limits, and controller of the robot used during training. Such a policy may fail to transfer even when a new object obeys the same joint structure or a new robot can produce the same object-level forces.

The interaction abstractions introduced in \cref{sec:interaction_abstractions} suggest a different organization. The learned policy should describe what must happen at the object---which force should be applied, given the object's articulation and current configuration---while a separate execution layer determines how a particular robot produces that force. Such a representation exploits the inherent structure of the task: it retains distinctions that govern the interaction, including whether the mechanism moves along a prismatic axis or about a revolute joint, while discarding differences in object identity and embodiment that do not change that structure.

This chapter develops that idea through \FLEX{} (Force-based Learning for EXtended Manipulation)~\citep{fang2025flex}, an object-centric force-space framework for manipulating articulated objects. \FLEX{} learns separate policies for single-degree-of-freedom prismatic and revolute mechanisms under quasi-static interaction. Each policy observes joint-relative object information and outputs a three-dimensional force at the contact point. Because training applies forces directly to simulated objects, no robot model is needed. At deployment, brief physical interaction estimates the joint type and parameters, after which an operational space controller maps the policy's desired forces to the commands of the available robot.

This decomposition creates a clear division of responsibility. The learned policy captures the physical structure shared across compatible articulated objects, whereas perception, parameter estimation, and control connect that policy to a particular object and embodiment. The experiments evaluate the consequences of this choice: training efficiency relative to robot-centric learning baselines, transfer from a small training set to 13 unseen articulated objects, and execution of the learned policies on Panda, UR5e, and Kinova Gen3 robots in simulation without retraining. A physical UR5 demonstration provides an additional test of sim-to-real transfer. The claim is intentionally bounded to the two supported articulation classes, quasi-static interaction, the evaluated estimation procedure, and robots capable of realizing the requested contact forces.

The remainder of the chapter reviews related work on sustained-contact manipulation, object-centric representations, and sim-to-real transfer. It then presents the \FLEX{} policy formulation, interactive joint-estimation procedure, and robot execution layer, followed by evaluations of learning efficiency, object generalization, cross-robot transfer, and physical deployment.
