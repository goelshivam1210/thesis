\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this dissertation is organized into five parts.

\textbf{Part I, Introduction and Preliminaries,} establishes the conceptual and formal foundations of the dissertation. \Cref{ch:novelty_open_world} introduces novelty in open-world autonomy by contrasting closed- and open-world assumptions, developing an agent-relative view of novelty as mismatch, and distinguishing assimilation from accommodation as two modes of response. It concludes by identifying the capabilities required of an open-world agent: detecting and characterizing mismatch, adapting from limited experience, revising representations when necessary, and integrating perception, reasoning, learning, and action. \Cref{ch:related_work} then reviews related work on adaptation under novelty. It organizes existing approaches according to whether they operate within a fixed representation or extend the agent's representational structure, considers foundation models as sources of broad priors and candidate structure, and identifies the gap addressed by this dissertation: localizable, action-grounded structural adaptation and generalization mediated by explicit abstractions. Finally, \cref{ch:foundations} develops the formalisms used in the technical chapters. It defines the agent--environment framework and the crafting running example, formalizes novelty and its taxonomies, introduces the planning and learning formalisms used throughout the dissertation, and explains how structured abstractions mediate interactions between an agent and its environment.

\textbf{Part II, Benchmarking Environments for Open Worlds,} provides the experimental foundation for studying novelty systematically. \Cref{ch:benchmarks} introduces benchmark environments, formalisms, and evaluation protocols that support the controlled injection of diverse forms of novelty and enable analysis of how agent performance degrades and recovers as conditions change.

\textbf{Part III, Adaptation,} studies how an agent can recover when novelty causes its existing knowledge or behaviors to fail. \Cref{ch:rapid_learn} presents a neurosymbolic cognitive architecture for handling novelty together with hybrid planning--learning methods that use structured action abstractions and counterfactual reasoning to localize execution failures and acquire missing behaviors. By targeting the failed components of a plan, these methods provide a form of localized behavioral accommodation without requiring the agent to relearn an entire task.

\textbf{Part IV, Generalization,} investigates how object-centric interaction abstractions expand the range of variation that can be assimilated within existing policy representations. \Cref{ch:flex} introduces \FLEX{}, an object-centric force-space framework for articulated-object manipulation, and demonstrates that policies learned in force space and utilizing the structure of object articulation can generalize across novel objects and transfer to different robot platforms without retraining or fine-tuning. \Cref{ch:pho} extends the force-space abstraction to rigid-body transport, presenting object-centric force policies for pushing heavy objects under unknown mass and friction and demonstrating zero-shot policy deployment from simulation on a legged mobile manipulator. Each technical chapter in Parts II--IV also reviews the work most closely related to its contribution and situates the proposed approach within that literature.

\textbf{Part V, Conclusion and Future Directions,} brings the contributions together. \Cref{ch:conclusion} summarizes the dissertation's main findings, presents a unified account of how structured abstractions mediate adaptation and generalization under novelty, and outlines future directions for autonomous agents and robots operating in open-world environments.
