\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this dissertation is organized as follows.

Chapter 2 presents the foundational concepts and formal framework used in the dissertation. We begin by formalizing the definition of novelty as a mismatch between an agent's internal models and the environment, followed by the taxonomy of novelties. We further present the preliminaries of the agent framework, which integrates symbolic planning and subsymbolic execution into a unified decision-making architecture. Finally, we discuss the role of structured abstractions in mediating the agent--environment interaction. The goal of this chapter is to establish the formal vocabulary and conceptual claims that the subsequent chapters build upon: benchmark environments for evaluating agents under controlled novelty (\cref{ch:benchmarks}), action abstractions that enable efficient adaptation under failure (\cref{ch:rapid_learn}), and interaction abstractions that support generalization across objects and embodiments (\cref{ch:generalization}).

 Chapter 3 is a broader literature review that situates the contributions of this dissertation within the landscape of open-world autonomy research. It reviews related work in the related disciplines that the current dissertation touches upon. Specifically, we review articles related to how open world autonomy research has evolved in the areas of Reinforcement Learning, and Hybrid Planning and Learning Systems, with used cases and applications in robotics. We also briefly review the recent advances in the area of foundation models and how they have impacted the design space for autonomous agents. Finally, we discuss how the contributions of this dissertation relate to and complement these broader developments, and identify open problems that remain.

Chapter 4 introduces benchmark environments, formalism, and evaluation methodologies for systematically studying novelty. These environments enable controlled injection of diverse classes of mismatch and provide metrics for analyzing how agent performance degrades and recovers under changing conditions. This chapter establishes an experimental foundation for evaluating adaptation and generalization in open-world settings.

Chapter 5 investigates adaptation under novelty. It presents a neurosymbolic cognitive architecture for open-world novelty handling and hybrid planning--learning methods that enable agents to recover from execution failures by leveraging structured action abstractions. These approaches utilize various counterfactual reasoning based methods to efficiently identify the source of failure and acquire missing behaviors, enabling agents to adapt to novel conditions without relearning entire tasks. By localizing failures to specific components of a plan and enabling targeted learning, these methods allow agents to efficiently acquire missing behaviors without relearning entire tasks.

Chapter 6 shows how abstractions defined at a certain level can lead to generalization in robotic manipulation. It presents object-centric, force-based learning approaches that represent interaction at the level of object dynamics. These representations enable learned skills to generalize across objects with similar semantics, across variations in physical properties, and across different robotic platforms, supporting robust performance under diverse conditions.

In Chapters 4 through 6, we also review related work specific to each technical contribution and discuss how the proposed methods compare to and complement existing approaches.

Chapter 7 concludes the dissertation by summarizing the main findings, articulating a unified perspective on how structured abstractions mediate an agent's response to novelty, and discussing future directions for research on autonomous agents and robots operating in open-world environments.
