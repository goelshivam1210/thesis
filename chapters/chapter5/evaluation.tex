\RAPidLearn{} was evaluated on a $12 \times 12$ \NovelGridworlds{} domain~\citep{goel2021novelgridworlds} with the pogostick crafting task described in \cref{sec:novelgridworlds} (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ngw_pogostick_screenshot}). The agent's sub-symbolic observation space consists of LiDAR-like sensors emitting beams at $\frac{\pi}{4}$ increments for each entity type (observation size $8 \times |\Entities|$), augmented with inventory and selected-item vectors. The action space includes navigation actions (\texttt{turn-left}, \texttt{turn-right}, \texttt{move-forward}), interaction actions (\texttt{break}, \texttt{extract-rubber}), crafting actions, and hierarchical operators (\texttt{approach-entity}) implemented by the internal planner.
\rev{
Five novelty scenarios were designed, spanning different types of environmental changes:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Axe-to-break (ATB) hard}: In this novelty, a novel entity \emph{axe} appears in the environment and the agent cannot break \emph{trees} without holding the \emph{axe}. We implement two versions of the novelty, easy and hard. In the easy version, the \emph{axe} is present in the agent's inventory, and the agent needs to learn to select the \emph{axe} and use it in front of the \emph{tree} to get \emph{tree-log} into its inventory. In the hard version, the \emph{axe} is present in the environment, and the agent needs to find it to get into its inventory and then use it to break \emph{trees}. We observe that the agent is able to generate executors that can reason about the affordances of new entities in the environment, i.e. learn to pick up the \emph{axe}, select it, and then \emph{break} the \emph{tree} by locating itself next to the \emph{tree}.
    
    \item \textbf{Fire-crafting-table (FCT) hard}: In this novelty, the agent cannot access the \emph{crafting-table} since its set on \emph{fire}. Two novel entities \emph{water} and \emph{fire} appears in the environment, and novel actions \emph{spray} and \emph{select-water} are added to the list of available actions. In order to access the \emph{crafting-table} the agent needs to select \emph{water} and use the action \emph{spray} in front of the \emph{crafting-table} in order to put it out from \emph{fire}, and hence successfully access it. In the easy version, the \emph{water} is already present in the agent's inventory and in the hard version \emph{water} is present in the environment and the agent needs to collect it to use it.
    The agent's sub-symbolic representation has information about the status of the \emph{fire} (on/off). 
    % The agent's learned executor in successfully dealing with more than one novel entities, and also novel predicates that are absent in the agent's knowledge base. 
    \item \textbf{Rubber-tree (RT) hard}: In this novelty, the agent cannot extract \emph{rubber} from the regular trees anymore, but a new entity \emph{rubber-tree} appears in the environment and the agent needs to use the \emph{rubber-tree} for \emph{rubber} extraction.
    We have two versions of this novelty, in the \emph{easy} version, the agent needs to select the \emph{tree-tap} and standing in front of a \emph{rubber-tree} it needs to use the action \emph{extract-rubber} to extract rubber. In the hard version, the agent's action space is augmented with a new action called \emph{place-tree-tap}, and in order to get rubber, the agent now needs to place the \emph{tree-tap} in front of the \emph{rubber-tree} and \emph{rubber} can only be extracted when the agent is in front of the placed \emph{tree-tap}. The agent's knowledge base does not have any \emph{in front of} predicate. The agent's executor learns the policy to place the new entity of \emph{tree-tap}, locate itself in front of it, and then extract \emph{rubber}. We observe that the agent is successful in synthesizing executors with predicates missing from the knowledge base.
    
    \item \textbf{Scrape-plank (SP)}: In this novelty, the \emph{break} action has no effect, and a novel action \emph{scrape-plank} is augmented in the agent's action space. The dynamics of the world changes in a way that the agent can no longer break \emph{trees} to get \emph{tree-logs}, but it can scrape planks from trees and using the action \emph{scrape-plank} in front of a \emph{tree} it gets $4$ \emph{planks} into its inventory. This novelty emphasizes on failure of effects of the operator rather than failure of the action itself. The executor learns a policy to generate \emph{planks}, by utilizing the \emph{scrape-plank} action to move ahead in the plan. Thus, the agent learns to tackle a novel scenario even when there are no new entities in the environment. 
    \item \textbf{ATB + FCT (easy)}: Two novelties are introduced simultaneously---axe-to-break and fire-crafting-table in their easy variants (items are pre-placed in inventory). This tests recovery from multiple independent operator failures.
\end{enumerate}}
\rev{
\subsubsection{Baselines}
\label{subsubsec:baselines}
We now describe how the baselines were designed and trained. In early experiments, the pre-novelty task with sparse rewards proved intractable for pure RL approaches. Moreover, the \RAPidLearn{} agent has access to the full PDDL description of the environment, which heavily biases any comparison in its favor. We therefore employ strong reward shaping to allow the RL baseline to learn the prenovelty task. A small reward ($+50$) is given for completing intermediate steps (e.g. crafting sticks or planks) when they are needed for the next major step in the crafting process. Larger rewards are given for the subgoals of crafting the treetap ($+200$), extracting rubber ($+300$) and finally crafting the pogostick ($+1000$). Note that the rewards are only given if the intermediate step is the appropriate thing to do next. I.e. no reward is given for breaking a tree, if the agent already has a treetap and enough sticks and planks in its inventory to craft the pogostick. In this case, a reward would only be given for obtaining the missing ingredient (rubber).
Making the reward conditional on the stage within the crafting process was essential in getting the agent to learn the pre-novelty task. In early versions of the reward shaping, we attempted to give reward for completing intermediate steps (approaching a tree, crafting sticks, etc.). This was exploited by the agent, which learned that the reward could be optimized by periodically turning away and re-approaching a tree until the end of its life.
}

\subsubsection{Experiments \& Results}
\rev{In this section, we describe the experiments we ran to answer the following questions. (Q1) How does the rapid-learn approach compare to the adapted transfer learning approaches in terms of the adaptation efficiency, and post adaptation success rates, compared across 5 novelty scenarios (Q2) How does each variant of knowledge-guided exploration component of Rapid-Learn fair, in terms of the adaptation efficiency across all novelty scenarios.}

\input{tables/chapter5/rapid_learn_results}
\input{figures/chapter5/rapid_learn_results_bars}

Three variants of \RAPidLearn{} were compared: \emph{RAPid-KGE-UCB} (with upper confidence bound action biasing), \emph{RAPid-KGE-UAB} (with uniform action biasing), and \emph{RAPid-EG} (with standard $\epsilon$-greedy exploration). Two transfer learning baselines were used: \emph{Actor-Critic Transfer}~\citep{clegg2017haptics}, which transfers both the policy and value function through PPO~\citep{schulman2017proximal}, and \emph{Policy Reuse}~\citep{glatt2017policy}, which transfers the learned policy. Both baselines were pre-trained on the base task with dense reward shaping until convergence ($>96\%$ success rate). The reinforcement learning component in all \RAPidLearn{} variants used the policy gradient algorithm~\citep{williams1992reinforce}. All results are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 10 independent trials.

\paragraph{Sample efficiency.} \Cref{tab:rapid_learn_results,fig:rapid_learn_curves} show that \RAPidLearn{} consistently adapts one to two orders of magnitude faster than the transfer learning baselines across all novelty scenarios.

In the axe-to-break hard novelty, RAPid-KGE-UCB adapts in approximately $2.35 \times 10^3$ time steps compared to $9.80 \times 10^4$ for actor-critic transfer and $5.57 \times 10^5$ for policy reuse---improvements of roughly $40\times$ and $230\times$, respectively. The advantage is most pronounced in the rubber-tree hard novelty, where both baselines fail to converge entirely, while all \RAPidLearn{} variants converge within $3.7 \times 10^4$ time steps. The sample efficiency advantage arises from two sources: the symbolic planner constrains the learning problem to a specific subgoal (the failed operator's effects), and knowledge-guided exploration focuses the agent on actions and states likely related to the novelty.

\paragraph{Effectiveness of knowledge-guided exploration.} The relative performance of the three \RAPidLearn{} variants reveals the contribution of knowledge-guided exploration. In complex novelties such as rubber-tree hard, where the agent must discover a spatial relation not in its symbolic knowledge, KGE-UCB and KGE-UAB provide measurable improvements in convergence speed over standard $\epsilon$-greedy exploration. In simpler novelties such as scrape-plank, where the solution involves a single novel action, the advantage of knowledge-guided exploration is less pronounced, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration can occasionally converge faster. This pattern is consistent with the expectation that knowledge-guided exploration is most valuable when the exploration space is large and the solution requires interacting with novel entities.

\paragraph{Multiple operator failures.} The ATB+FCT easy scenario demonstrates that \RAPidLearn{} handles multiple simultaneous novelties effectively. The agent learns two independent executors---one for each failed operator---and achieves a 97\% post-novelty success rate in approximately $4 \times 10^3$ time steps. The rubber-tree hard scenario further demonstrates recovery from cascading failures: after learning to extract rubber from the rubber-tree, the agent discovers that it can no longer break the now-unbreakable rubber-tree, triggering a second executor discovery process. Despite this implicit multiple-failure structure, \RAPidLearn{} converges successfully while the baselines fail entirely.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.} Unpaired $t$-tests between the best-performing \RAPidLearn{} variant and each baseline confirm that the differences in post-novelty success rate are statistically significant ($p < 0.01$) in all scenarios except scrape-plank versus policy reuse ($p = 0.06$), where the baselines achieve comparable final performance albeit at substantially higher sample cost.

\paragraph{Ablation: hierarchical actions.} An ablation study removing hierarchical actions (the \texttt{approach} operator) from the learner's action space shows that their inclusion substantially improves sample efficiency, particularly for complex novelties. Without hierarchical actions, RAPid-EG fails to converge on the fire-crafting-table hard novelty, while RAPid-KGE-UCB still converges but requires more episodes. This confirms that composing learned behaviors with existing executors is a key advantage of the hybrid architecture.

\rev{\subsection{Evaluation Setting}}
\label{sec:rl_eval_setting}

\rev{The component-wise and systemic evaluations use the two environments shown in \cref{fig:snapshots}. The primary environment is the \emph{Polycraft World} domain~\citep{goss2023polycraft}, a partially observable, multi-agent ``Minecraft mod'' in which the agent competes with other actors for resources to craft a pogostick. Completing the task requires a sequence of sub-tasks: mining trees, diamond, and platinum; trading with \textit{trader} actors; crafting intermediate objects such as a tree tap; placing the tree tap on a tree and collecting rubber; collecting a key from a chest; opening a door and navigating to another room to find a safe; unlocking the safe; and finally crafting the pogostick. Each trial instantiates two or three rooms of varying sizes, a rival pogostick-building actor (the \textit{pogoist}), two \textit{trader} actors, and resources scattered throughout. The agent starts with an iron axe and perceives only the room it currently occupies. We additionally use the \NovelGridworlds{} environment~\citep{goel2021novelgridworlds} for component-level evaluations that require a tight development loop, since many Polycraft novelties were deliberately concealed from the architecture team.}

\rev{\paragraph{Novelty categories.} The systemic evaluation uses ten broad categories of novelty, summarized in \cref{tab:novelty_category}. These categories are orthogonal to the four-way functional taxonomy of \cref{sec:novelty_typology}: they organize novelties by \emph{what} changes in the world (objects, actors, relations, events, etc.), whereas the functional taxonomy organizes them by \emph{how} the change affects the agent's plans. In total, the evaluation includes 288 novelties across the ten categories, of which 216 were known during the development of the agent.}

\rev{\input{tables/chapter5/novelty_category}}

\rev{\paragraph{Tournament protocol.} Evaluations are organized into \emph{tournaments}. Each novelty scenario is associated with three tournaments, and each tournament comprises 50 task episodes: the first $n$ episodes (with $n$ chosen randomly between 5 and 20) are drawn from the pre-novelty distribution, and the remaining episodes contain the novelty. Three additional no-novelty tournaments of 50 episodes each are included as controls. In each episode the agent earns a positive reward for completing the task and a negative cost per action; failing the task yields a score of zero. The episode in which the agent first reports novelty is recorded. A non-novelty-aware fast-forward planning agent, otherwise unrelated to the evaluated agent, provides reference scores; it performs within 15\% of the evaluated agent on pre-novelty episodes and degrades significantly on post-novelty episodes.}

\rev{\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.} Novelty handling is measured with five tournament-level metrics:}
\rev{\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{False Negatives before detection ($\text{FN}_\text{CDT}$)}: the average number of post-novelty episodes elapsed before the agent reports novelty. A perfect score of $0$ means novelty is reported in the first post-novelty episode.
    \item \emph{Correctly Detected Trials (CDT\%)}: the percentage of trials in which the agent reports novelty after (and not before) it is introduced.
    \item \emph{False Positives (FP\%)}: the percentage of trials in which the agent reports novelty before it is introduced.
    \item \emph{Novelty Reaction Performance (NRP\%)}: the average post-novelty task score of the agent divided by the average pre-novelty task score of the non-novelty-aware control agent.
    \item \emph{Goal Achieved (GA\%)}: the percentage of post-novelty tasks in a tournament in which the agent achieves the goal.
\end{itemize}}

\rev{Agent implementation details, bounded exploration heuristics, and compute resources for the systemic evaluation are provided in \cref{sec:ch5_agent_implementation,sec:ch6_compute}.}


\rev{\subsection{Executor Learner Component Evaluation}}
\label{sec:rl_eval_components}

\rev{We evaluate the executor learner in isolation from the other neural components of the architecture (the vision and agent models, which are not the focus of this dissertation). The evaluation uses four \NovelGridworlds{} scenarios purpose-built to require reinforcement learning---stochasticity (\emph{This is just random}), dynamic behavior (\emph{Convince me}), spatial relations (\emph{Sapling can't grow here!}), and action sequences (\emph{Show me your card first})---none of which can be resolved by symbolic exploration alone. We compare the Base agent (symbolic inference only) against Base+Executor Learner. Each tournament comprises 50 episodes, each capped at 600 time steps or 15 minutes, and the goal-achieved rate (GA\%) is averaged over 10 independent trials. The executor learner explores and learns online during evaluation and is randomly initialized for each run.}

\rev{\input{tables/chapter5/executor_learner_component}}

\rev{\Cref{tab:executor_learner_component} shows that the Base+Executor Learner agent substantially outperforms the Base agent across all four scenarios. On \emph{Convince me}, where the agent must learn to favor repeated interaction with a specific trader, the Base agent fails entirely ($0\%$) while the executor learner succeeds $59\%$ of the time. On the \emph{Show me your card first} scenario, which requires learning a novel \emph{sequence} of operators (retrieving an accreditation card from the safe before trading), the Base agent again fails completely while the executor learner succeeds $21\%$ of the time---the search space of operator sequences is intractable for the enumerative symbolic strategies but tractable for the learner's flexible neural representation and knowledge-guided exploration. The smaller improvement on \emph{Sapling can't grow here!} reflects an implementation limitation discussed below.}

\rev{\subsubsection{Scenario Walkthroughs}}
\label{sec:ch5_executor_walkthroughs}

\rev{We provide a brief description of each \NovelGridworlds{} scenario from \cref{tab:executor_learner_component}.}

\rev{\paragraph{This is just random (stochasticity).} The \texttt{break} operator no longer works on oak logs; instead, breaking any other block has a $10\%$ chance of also dropping an oak log. The executor learner solves the task $21\%$ of the time versus $2\%$ for the symbolic explorer, demonstrating adaptation to stochastic dynamics.}

\rev{\paragraph{Convince me (dynamic behavior).} Resources are scarce, and interacting with a specific trader more than three times yields extra items. The Base agent fails entirely, while the executor learner learns to favor repeated interaction and solves the task nearly $60\%$ of the time.}

\rev{\paragraph{Sapling can't grow here! (spatial relation).} A sapling can no longer be placed within three blocks of any tree or wall. The executor learner helps only about $10\%$ of the time; because it operates over the same high-level operators as the Base agent and lacks access to low-level navigation actions, it cannot fully learn the spatial rule---an implementation limitation rather than a limitation of the approach.}

\rev{\paragraph{Show me your card first (action sequence).} The agent must first collect an accreditation card from the safe before it is authorized to trade. The space of operator \emph{sequences} is intractable for the enumerative symbolic strategies, which never accommodate the novelty; the executor learner, with its flexible neural representation and knowledge-guided exploration, succeeds $21\%$ of the time.}


\subsection{Systemic Evaluation}
\label{sec:rl_eval_architecture}

To evaluate the full neurosymbolic architecture, a large-scale systemic evaluation was conducted in the Polycraft World domain~\citep{goss2023polycraft} using the tournament protocol and metrics of \cref{sec:rl_eval_setting}. The evaluation spanned all 288 novelties across the ten categories, comprising 864 novelty tournaments plus three no-novelty tournaments. Of the 288 novelties, 216 were known during architecture development; the remaining 72 were \emph{concealed novelties} designed by a separate evaluation team behind a novelty firewall and never shared with the architecture developers. \rev{Because of the high computational cost of the systemic evaluation, a single set of experiments is run per configuration.}

\rev{We evaluate four agent configurations in the accommodation-capable systemic evaluation, plus a vision-only baseline for detection-only comparison. The \emph{Base} agent uses only symbolic inference and the symbolic novelty-exploration strategies, excluding the executor learner. \emph{Base+Agent Model} augments the base with neural behavioral modeling of other actors. \emph{Base+Executor Learner} augments the base with the \RAPidLearn{} executor learner, invoked only after all symbolic strategies fail within a preset time limit. \emph{Base+Agent Model+Executor Learner} combines all components. The vision-only configuration is included for completeness from the AIJ evaluation but is not a focus of this dissertation.}

\input{tables/chapter5/polycraft_overall}

\paragraph{Novelty detection.} \Cref{tab:polycraft_overall} shows that configurations including the agent model achieve better novelty detection performance, with the combined configuration reaching 88.7\% correctly detected trials and a false negative rate of 0.39 episodes. The agent model is particularly effective for action and goal novelties, where actors exhibit changed behavior that symbolic inference alone cannot detect. \rev{This gain comes with higher variance in the false-positive rate, since the agent model is the only learning-based detection component and small random effects in the environment can trigger spurious detections.} \rev{We report these results for completeness as part of the integrated architecture evaluation; the vision and agent models are supporting components of the AIJ system rather than the primary contributions of this dissertation.}

\paragraph{Novelty accommodation.} \rev{\Cref{tab:base_metrics,tab:agent_model_metrics,tab:rl_systemic_results,tab:combined_systemic_results} break performance down by novelty category for each accommodation-capable configuration.} The Base+Executor Learner configuration achieves the highest novelty accommodation performance, with 66.6\% goal achievement rate and 74.2\% novelty reaction performance, outperforming all other configurations. \rev{We note that the NRP metric may disadvantage reinforcement-learning agents, since it penalizes the extended exploration that learning requires; the measurable NRP improvement of the executor learner despite this bias underscores its positive effect on accommodation.} The executor learner proves most valuable in attribute novelties (e.g., learning to break birch trees that fail to produce rubber) and concealed novelties where symbolic exploration strategies are insufficient. A notable example is the ``different items in chests'' novelty, where the base agent fails because it assumes all chests contain identical items, while the executor learner discovers a policy to open all chests and collect diverse ingredients.

\paragraph{Interaction effects.} The combined Base+Agent Model+Executor Learner configuration achieves the best detection performance but lower accommodation scores than the Base+Executor Learner alone. \rev{As \cref{tab:combined_systemic_results} shows, this deficit is spread across categories rather than concentrated in one.} This arises from an unforeseen interaction: the executor learner's exploration changes the environment state, which triggers false novelty detections from the agent model, leading to unnecessary exploration that hinders task performance. This finding highlights the challenge of integrating multiple adaptive components and suggests that coordination mechanisms between detection and accommodation modules are an important direction for future work.

\paragraph{Known versus concealed novelties.} \rev{\Cref{tab:known_systemic_results,tab:unknown_systemic_results} separate the results for the known and concealed novelty sets. The executor learner showed a measurable improvement on concealed novelties ($68.2\%$ NRP and $61.3\%$ GA for Base+Executor Learner, versus $64.8\%$ NRP and $57.1\%$ GA for the Base agent), confirming that learning-based accommodation generalizes to scenarios not anticipated during development. The configurations that include the agent model perform proportionally better on the concealed set because, owing to the development cycle, the concealed set is drawn from the Environments, Goals, and Events categories and contains proportionally more actor-related novelties that the agent model detects well. This is a strong validation of the architecture's open-world capabilities: the reinforcement learning component discovers recovery behaviors for novelties that neither the system designers nor the symbolic exploration strategies could have foreseen.}

\rev{\emph{Must break tree tap to get rubber (attribute novelty).} The operator to collect rubber from a tree tap stops working, and the agent must instead break the tree tap to obtain rubber. The Base agent's total-effect-failure recovery only applies operator variation to single-parameter operators, so the two-parameter variation \texttt{break(tree tap, tree)} is never tried and the agent gives up---except in the rare case where the rival \textit{pogoist} chops down the tree, causing a precondition failure that leads the agent to break the tree tap and serendipitously discover the rubber effect. This case illustrates both the flexibility and the brittleness of purely symbolic recovery.}

\rev{\emph{Cannot collect rubber from birch trees (attribute novelty).} A new birch tree subtype is introduced from which rubber cannot be collected. When the Base agent attempts to collect from a birch, the total-effect-failure policy again fails because operator variation is restricted to single-parameter operators. The Base+Executor Learner agent instead learns a surprising recovery policy: it breaks all birch trees in the environment, forcing the symbolic planner to collect rubber from oak trees, which have no such restriction. This unexpected strategy---removing the sources of failure so that the planner is compelled toward a working solution---showcases the flexibility of the executor learner in integrating with the symbolic planner, since its reward function only requires reaching a state from which a valid plan exists.}

\rev{\emph{New item needed to craft pogostick (object novelty).} A new item (gold), obtainable by mining a previously unknown block, is added to the pogostick recipe. Before planning, the goal manager detects the novel block by comparing its knowledge base to the observed world. Knowledge discovery generates subgoals involving the novel object; the subgoal \texttt{break(gold)} deposits gold into the inventory, and the exploration component creates a new operator recording this effect. The agent then plans to mine enough gold and completes the task.}

\rev{\emph{New supplier actor (agent novelty).} A previously unknown \textit{supplier} appears that hands over a pogostick when interacted with; in this variant the pogostick can no longer be crafted, so the supplier is the only source. During knowledge discovery, the agent interacts with the supplier, observes the unexpected effect of receiving a pogostick, and creates a new \texttt{interactWith(supplier)} operator. In subsequent games it plans to obtain the pogostick immediately, exploiting this beneficial novelty.}

\rev{\emph{Traders spawn in side rooms (relation novelty).} The \textit{traders} no longer appear in the starting room but in a side room, so their existence is initially unknown. Because trading is required to solve the task, the agent cannot plan and encounters a prohibitive novelty. The prohibitive-novelty recovery policy executes exploratory operators such as \texttt{exploreRooms()}, entering the side rooms, discovering the traders, obtaining their trade offers, and generating new trade operators, after which the agent replans and succeeds.}

\rev{\emph{Different items in chests (object novelty).} The pogostick ingredients are scattered among identical chests and are unobtainable otherwise. The Base agent assumes all chests contain the same item, discovers rubber in one via the partial-effect-failure policy, but still cannot plan for the remaining ingredients and gives up. The Base+Executor Learner agent instead explores with reinforcement learning, prioritizing the newly created chest operator, eventually collects ingredients from all the chests, and replans to solve the task---an example of the learner overcoming a representational limitation of the symbolic reasoner.}
