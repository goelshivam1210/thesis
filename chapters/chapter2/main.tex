\chapter{Related Work: Novelty, Change, and Open-World Autonomy}
\label{ch:related_work}
\label{ch:perspectives}

Autonomous agents deployed outside controlled settings face a common difficulty: their world models are inevitably incomplete, and the world will eventually behave in ways those models did not anticipate. This dissertation develops methods for agents that remain effective under such conditions. This chapter situates that goal in the broader literature.

The chapter proceeds in five steps. It begins with accounts of novelty and adaptation in psychology, neuroscience, and cognitive science (\cref{sec:novelty_perspectives}). It then examines how artificial intelligence formalizes learning under change, emphasizing the distinctions between open-world and open-ended settings and between distributional drift and structural novelty (\cref{sec:landscape_of_change}). Next, it reviews how deployed agents respond to mismatch and where those responses fall short (\cref{sec:capabilities_and_gaps}), assesses foundation models as both a resource and a foil (\cref{sec:foundation_models}), and closes by positioning the dissertation's contributions against the field's current trajectory (\cref{sec:synthesis_positioning}).

Throughout, concrete examples distinguish changes that can be absorbed within an existing representation from changes that require the representation itself to be extended. The formal vocabulary---novelty, the agent framework, and structured abstraction---is developed in \cref{ch:foundations}; here, related concepts are introduced descriptively and made precise later. The technical chapters (\cref{ch:benchmarks,ch:rapid_learn,ch:generalization}) each include focused related-work discussions specific to their contributions; this chapter provides the overarching context.
