\chapter{Background and Related Work: Novelty in Open-World Autonomy}
\label{ch:related_work}
\label{ch:perspectives}

In this chapter, we survey and synthesize how novelty and adaptation have been understood across cognitive science and artificial intelligence.
We begin by developing the idea of novelty as an agent-relative mismatch, drawing on work on novelty, surprise, predictive processing, and the big world hypothesis to explain why novelty depends on the representations an agent brings to the world.
Next, we introduce assimilation and accommodation as two modes of response to mismatch, distinguishing changes that can be handled within an existing representation from changes that require representational revision.

We then use this distinction to organize related work on concept drift, continual learning, open-set recognition, planning, creative problem solving, open-world AI, and foundation models.
Collectively, the work reviewed in this chapter positions the dissertation within the broader open-world autonomy literature and motivates the role of structured abstractions in adaptation and generalization.
The chapter is organized around the following question: what kinds of novelty can an agent handle within its current representation, and what kinds require the representation itself to change?

The formal vocabulary for this argument---novelty, the agent framework, and structured abstraction---is developed in \cref{ch:foundations}.
Here the same ideas are introduced at the level of related work.
The technical chapters provide contribution-specific comparisons; this chapter supplies the broader case for why structured abstractions matter when agents face worlds their models do not fully cover.
