\chapter{Novelty in Open-World Autonomy}
\label{ch:novelty_open_world}
\label{ch:perspectives}

This chapter defines the open-world problem setting that motivates the dissertation.
We begin by contrasting closed-world assumptions with open-world autonomy, where an agent's knowledge and representational vocabulary are necessarily incomplete with respect to the environment.
We then develop the idea of novelty as an agent-relative mismatch, drawing on work on novelty, surprise, predictive processing, and the big world hypothesis to explain why novelty depends on the representations an agent brings to the world.

With this conceptual view in place, we introduce a taxonomy of novelty by task impact and by domain element.
Finally, we use the distinction between assimilation and accommodation to explain why some changes can be handled within an existing representation while others require representational revision.
The chapter closes by identifying the requirements that open-world agents must satisfy: detecting and characterizing mismatch, adapting from limited experience, revising representations when needed, and integrating perception, reasoning, learning, and action.
