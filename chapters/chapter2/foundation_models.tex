\section{Foundation Models: Breadth and Its Limits}
\label{sec:foundation_models}

No account of open-world autonomy today is complete without foundation models: large pre-trained language, vision, and vision--language--action models whose broad priors promise generalization without task-specific engineering. They are both a resource and a foil. Their breadth is real. Recent surveys catalogue rapid uptake across robotic perception, planning, and control, from simulation-driven design to open-world execution~\citep{khan2025foundation}, and vision--language--action models now integrate perception, language, and action in a single system, with dozens of variants appearing in only a few years~\citep{sapkota2025vla}. The same surveys, however, are candid about the limits that matter here: grounding, resilience, embodiment, and trust remain open problems~\citep{khan2025foundation}, and generalization to genuinely unseen tasks and across embodiments is repeatedly identified as unresolved~\citep{sapkota2025vla}. The thesis-relevant point is that breadth is not the same as \emph{diagnosable, generalizable structure}, the property needed for open-world recovery.

Two limitations bear directly on this dissertation's argument. The first is that foundation models do not reliably perform \emph{structured} reasoning even when the structure is explicit. \citet{gobel2026agentic} compare a language model, used both directly and as an agent with a stepwise PDDL simulation engine, against Fast Downward on Blocksworld. The classical planner solves about $85\%$ of instances, while the language-model approaches solve roughly two-thirds ($63.7\%$ direct, $66.7\%$ agentic); the agentic variant adds only a few points at $5.7\times$ the token cost, and produced plans appear more consistent with training-data recall than with generalizable planning. Far from showing that structure rescues language-model planning, the result underscores that broad priors do not substitute for compositional search, supporting the structured representations this dissertation builds on. The second limitation is creative problem solving: generating genuinely new concepts when existing ones do not suffice. \citet{nair2024cps} argue, as a position supported by preliminary experiments, that large language and vision models lack this capacity and propose integrating computational-creativity principles to supply it.

The most promising direction, and the one closest to this dissertation, is hybrid: use a foundation model's knowledge to \emph{propose} structure, then let symbolic and learning components verify and execute it. \citet{lu2026novelty} instantiate this pattern for novelty adaptation. A language model identifies the operator missing after a novelty, a symbolic planner incorporates it, and the language model writes a reward function that guides reinforcement learning to acquire the corresponding control policy, outperforming prior methods at both operator discovery and operator learning in continuous domains. \citet{lorang2025fewshot} similarly learn symbolic abstractions and diffusion-policy controllers jointly from roughly five demonstrations, with a high-level oracle restricting each controller to a \emph{minimal observation and action space}---an abstraction move central to \cref{ch:generalization}. These systems are the natural point of comparison for \cref{ch:rapid_learn}, which addresses the same executor-discovery problem. They show that a language model can stand in for parts of the recovery pipeline while sharing this dissertation's premise: structured, localizable representations are what make adaptation efficient and interpretable.
