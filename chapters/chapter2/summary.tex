\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch2_summary}

This chapter introduced the conceptual problem setting for the dissertation.
Open-world autonomy differs from closed-world decision making because an agent's models, categories, actions, and assumptions may be incomplete with respect to the environment in which it acts.
Novelty was defined as an agent-relative mismatch between the world and the agent's current memory, expectations, models, or representational vocabulary.
This view explains why the same change may be routine for one agent and disruptive for another.

The chapter also distinguished assimilation from accommodation.
Some changes can be absorbed by updating parameters, predictions, or policies within an existing representation.
Other changes require representational revision: adding a category, relation, precondition, action, executor, or abstraction that was previously missing.
This distinction leads to four requirements for open-world autonomy: agents must detect and characterize mismatch, adapt from limited experience, revise representations when needed, and integrate perception, reasoning, learning, and action.
The next chapter uses these requirements as a lens for organizing related work and identifying the gap addressed by the dissertation.
