\section{Where This Dissertation Fits}
\label{sec:synthesis_positioning}
\label{sec:open_world_landscape}
\label{sec:related_work_positioning}
\label{sec:perspectives_integration}
\label{sec:ch2_summary}

The threads of this chapter converge on a single claim. Novelty is a relation---a mismatch between an agent's models and its world---and adaptive systems resolve it in two modes: assimilation, which absorbs change within existing structure, and accommodation, which restructures when absorption fails (\cref{sec:novelty_perspectives}).

Artificial intelligence has largely automated the first mode. Concept drift, continual learning, and meta-RL adjust parameters within a fixed representational vocabulary, and are powerful precisely there (\cref{sec:landscape_of_change}). The change open worlds impose is often the second kind: structural novelty that alters the vocabulary itself. The same boundary appears independently as the distinction between out-of-distribution and out-of-design-scope situations~\citep{wray2025requirements}, and as the strong form of open-world learning~\citep{kejriwal2024challenges}.

Surveying how deployed agents respond to mismatch (\cref{sec:capabilities_and_gaps}) reveals a consistent trade-off: methods that grow an agent's representation tend to do so slowly and globally, while methods that act quickly tend to leave the representation fixed. Foundation models widen the priors an agent brings to novelty, but they do not by themselves supply the diagnosable structure that recovery needs (\cref{sec:foundation_models}).

The current trajectory of the field reinforces, rather than closes, this gap. Forward-looking assessments of AI and neuroscience name brittleness under novelty and weak physical-world interaction as central unmet challenges~\citep{zador2025neuroai}. They also argue that embodied competence is not captured by language ability alone: an embodied system must process high-rate sensory input and produce high-rate action over long horizons, a capacity language models lack~\citep{tellex2026grounded}. Even mature deep RL for robotics remains bounded by the cost and difficulty of physical interaction~\citep{tang2025deep}. At the field level, \citet{roy2021machine} make the broader point forcefully: embodied intelligence does not follow straightforwardly from standard machine learning because deployed agents must operate under conditions unlike training and adapt readily when those conditions change.

Against this backdrop, the contribution of this dissertation is specific and foundational. Rather than building a full-stack open-world agent, it studies how \emph{structured abstractions} let an agent respond to structural novelty with fast, localized accommodation.

The dissertation develops this idea in two complementary ways. Action abstractions localize failure and bound what must be relearned (\cref{ch:rapid_learn}); interaction abstractions make entire classes of object and embodiment variation fall within existing competence rather than register as novelty at all (\cref{ch:generalization}); and the benchmarks in \cref{ch:benchmarks} inject structural novelty under controlled conditions so these dynamics can be evaluated.

The two examples illustrate the distinction. In the axe example, the important fact is not simply that performance drops after a change, but that the failed plan step identifies where the agent's executor or model is incomplete. In the manipulation examples of \cref{ch:generalization}, the complementary lesson is that a better abstraction can prevent object and embodiment changes from becoming novel in the first place.

Where DREAM restructures slowly and globally, and drift methods re-estimate within a fixed vocabulary, this dissertation occupies the space between: extending an agent's representation exactly where the world has invalidated it, and no more. The formal apparatus that makes this claim precise---the definition of novelty, the agent framework, and the notion of abstraction that ties them together---is developed in \cref{ch:foundations}.
