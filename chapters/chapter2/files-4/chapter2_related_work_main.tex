\chapter{Related Work and Perspectives on Open-World Autonomy}
\label{ch:related_work}

Autonomous agents that must operate outside controlled settings face a common difficulty: their models of the world are inevitably incomplete, and the world will, sooner or later, behave in ways those models did not anticipate. This dissertation develops methods for agents that remain effective under such conditions. This chapter situates that goal within the broader research landscape, spanning three levels. It begins with how novelty and adaptation have been understood across psychology, neuroscience, and cognitive science (\cref{sec:novelty_perspectives}), which provides conceptual grounding for the problem. It then reviews how artificial intelligence has formalized learning under change---reinforcement learning, continual and transfer learning, concept-drift adaptation, and intrinsic motivation---and draws the distinction between distributional change and structural novelty that motivates the dissertation (\cref{sec:computational_open_world}). Finally, it turns to the open-world autonomy subfield specifically, reviewing work on sensing, planning and learning, and execution and recovery (\cref{sec:open_world_sensing,sec:open_world_planning_learning,sec:open_world_execution_recovery}), the growing role of foundation models in reshaping the design space (\cref{sec:foundation_models}), and how the contributions of this dissertation relate to and complement these developments (\cref{sec:related_work_positioning}).

The formal vocabulary used throughout the dissertation---the definition of novelty, the agent framework, and the notion of structured abstraction---is developed in \cref{ch:foundations}; here, related concepts are introduced descriptively and made precise there. The technical chapters (\cref{ch:benchmarks,ch:rapid_learn,ch:generalization}) additionally include focused related-work discussions specific to each contribution; this chapter provides the overarching context that ties them together.
