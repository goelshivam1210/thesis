% I was in the fifth semester of my undergraduate education when I chose an elective called \textit{Soft Computing and Fuzzy Logic}. In one of the textbooks, I came across a line: ``Machines can think.'' That line sparked a curiosity in me that eventually led me down this path. At the time, I could never have imagined that I would go on to pursue a PhD in Computer Science and spend so many years learning about artificial intelligence and robotics. Ironically, I still believe that machines cannot think---at least not yet!---but perhaps it took me a PhD to arrive at that realization.

% Since then, I have been fortunate enough to keep following that curiosity. What began as an interest in thinking machines gradually took me toward acting robots, and this journey eventually became a PhD focused on AI and robotics. Along the way, I met many wonderful people who shaped the way I think and taught me lessons that extend far beyond research---lessons about discipline, rigor, responsibility, and accountability in one's work. This dissertation would not have been possible without them.

A single line — “Machines can think”— set me on a journey from a curious undergraduate to a researcher in AI and robotics. Years later, the question remains open, but I am deeply grateful to everyone who taught me how to think about it more carefully.

I am deeply grateful to my advisor, Professor Jivko Sinapov, for giving me the freedom to pursue the questions that genuinely interested me while guiding me toward those that truly mattered. He helped me develop a practical intuition for research, encouraged me to engage directly and hands-on with robotics, and taught me to ask the right questions. His mentorship shaped my desire to pursue intellectually novel ideas rather than settle for incremental progress.

I would also like to thank Professor Matthias Scheutz for his trust in me and for continually pushing me to hold myself and my work to a higher standard. His emphasis on finding simple, principled solutions — and on looking beyond the hype surrounding new ideas — has had a lasting influence on how I think about and conduct research. His rigor, high expectations, and confidence in my abilities challenged me to grow into a more careful and capable researcher.

I am deeply indebted to my committee members, Professor Reuth Mirsky and Professor Chris Rogers, for their thoughtful comments and for giving their invaluable time to help shape this dissertation.

I also owe special thanks to Professor Matthew Taylor, who introduced me to research and remained a generous mentor throughout this journey. His feedback greatly strengthened my work, while his kindness and humility taught me lessons that extend far beyond research.

Research is rarely an individual endeavor, and I was fortunate to collaborate with Yash Shukla, Vasanth Sarathy, Panagiotis Lymperopoulos, James Staley, Pierrick Lorang, Gyan Tatiya, and Timothy Duggan. Their ideas, perspectives, and technical rigor made me a better researcher, and the lessons I learned from them will remain with me.

I am also grateful to the master’s and undergraduate students I had the opportunity to collaborate with and mentor: Yichen Wei, Bharat Kesari, Shijie Fang, Wenchang Gao, Klara Chura, and Tomas Maranga. Mentoring them became an important part of my own growth, and I learned as much from the experience as I hope they did.

My years at Tufts would not have been the same without my labmates and friends from the MULIP, HRI, and AABL labs, as well as the wider robotics community. I am especially thankful to Evana, Andre, Tanushree, Brennan, Si, Julia, Hang, Jindan, Mitchell, and Katie for the conversations, conference memories, coffee-shop visits, lunches, and countless moments of friendship that I will always cherish.

I also thank the Tufts Computer Science Department, particularly Sarah Richmond, Johny Redmond, and Sandy Schulberg, for their invaluable support and for helping me navigate the many administrative complexities of graduate school. I would be remiss not to acknowledge the robots that accompanied me along the way— Boston Dynamics SPOTs (Phi and Psi), LoCoBot, and UR5. They often tested my patience, but they also taught me a great deal.

Beyond the university, I was fortunate to have the support of Saurabh Pant, Anmol Vishwakarma, Binita Saha, Saurabh Gupta, Santosh Swain, and many many more friends I made along this wonderful journey. Their friendship carried me through difficult moments and reminded me that there was always a world beyond the PhD.
I would also like to mention Jiddu Krishnamurthy, David Bohm and Kevin Liu whose writings and philosphy helped me explore my constant quest of understanding the nature of reality. Their readings have helped me develop a worldview of how a human's action without any expectations can be purest form of action, subsequently leading to empathy without any desire can make our world a better place.

Everything in this dissertation rests on the love, sacrifice, and steady support of my family. I thank my mother for listening with endless patience and for being my constant source of comfort. I thank my father for giving me the courage to take risks, knowing that I would always have his support. I am especially grateful to my younger brother, Satyam, whose belief in me has always been a source of strength. This dissertation is dedicated to them.
